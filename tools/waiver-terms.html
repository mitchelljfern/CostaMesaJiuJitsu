3. The risks are real

Brazilian jiu jitsu is a full-contact combat sport. Training partners apply joint locks, chokes and strangles, pins, takedowns and throws to each other, often at full resistance. I understand the Activities have inherent risks that cannot be removed without changing the nature of the sport, including:

  • Sprains, strains, torn ligaments and tendons, dislocations and broken bones, especially to the knees, ankles, elbows, shoulders, fingers, toes, ribs, neck and back, including injuries from submissions applied too fast or released too late;
  • Loss of consciousness from chokes and strangles, and injury to the throat, neck and blood vessels;
  • Concussions and other head injuries, injuries to the spine and spinal cord, paralysis, and eye, ear (including "cauliflower ear"), nose and teeth injuries;
  • Injuries from falls, slams, collisions with other people, walls or objects, and from being landed on by a heavier partner;
  • Skin infections and contagious illnesses spread by close contact or shared mats and equipment, such as ringworm, impetigo, staph and MRSA, herpes gladiatorum, colds, flu and COVID-19;
  • Overexertion, dehydration, heat illness, heart attack, sudden cardiac arrest, stroke and, in rare cases, permanent disability or death.

These risks can come from my own actions or inactions; from training partners and other people, including partners who are less experienced, bigger, stronger or more aggressive than me or who make mistakes; from the instruction, supervision or techniques taught; from the condition of the facility, mats or equipment; and from the ordinary negligence of the Released Parties.

Concussion and Head Injury Information Sheet

A concussion is a brain injury caused by a bump, blow or jolt to the head, or by a hit to the body that makes the head move quickly back and forth. In jiu jitsu this can happen from takedowns and throws, the head hitting the mat, or collisions with other people. Most concussions happen without passing out. Even a "ding" can be serious.

Signs others may see: appears dazed or stunned; is confused about instructions or forgets what they were doing; moves clumsily; answers slowly; loses consciousness, even briefly; shows mood, behavior or personality changes; cannot remember what happened before or after the hit.

Symptoms the athlete may report: headache or pressure in the head; nausea or vomiting; dizziness or balance problems; double or blurry vision; sensitivity to light or noise; feeling sluggish, hazy, foggy or groggy; trouble concentrating or remembering; confusion; or "just not feeling right."

Call 911 right away for: one pupil larger than the other; drowsiness or not being able to wake up; a headache that gets worse and does not go away; slurred speech, weakness, numbness or decreased coordination; repeated vomiting; seizures; very unusual behavior or agitation; or any loss of consciousness that does not quickly resolve.

What to do: stop training immediately, tell a coach, and see a licensed health care provider. Rest is key. Returning too soon, while the brain is still healing, risks a second, more serious injury. Learn more at the CDC's HEADS UP program: cdc.gov/heads-up.

Sudden Cardiac Arrest Information Sheet

Sudden cardiac arrest is when the heart suddenly stops beating effectively. It is different from a heart attack. It is rare in young people but can happen during exercise, often because of a heart condition no one knew about. Without CPR and an AED within minutes, it is usually fatal.

Warning signs to report to a coach and a doctor: fainting or a seizure during or right after exercise; chest pain or discomfort with exercise; unusual shortness of breath; a racing, pounding or fluttering heartbeat; dizziness or lightheadedness during exercise; or getting tired much more easily than expected. Also tell a doctor about any family history of heart conditions or of sudden, unexplained death before age 50.

What the Gym does: anyone who faints or shows warning signs is removed from training and needs clearance from a licensed health care provider before returning. In an emergency, coaches call 911, start hands-only CPR, and use an AED if one is available. Learn more from the California Department of Education at cde.ca.gov/ci/pl/scaprevention.asp.
